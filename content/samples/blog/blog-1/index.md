The rapid evolution of technology allowed personal computers to gradually replace numerous task-specific machines, including those used in music production. As soon as it became possible, the concept of a recording studio got reimagined as a DAW (Digital Audio Workstation), packing almost all your fancy studio gear into a single shortcut on your computer screen. The ever-growing market of VST plugins saturated the picture even more, offering more virtual instruments and effects you knew existed. More often than not, musicians and producers began using state-of-the-art mixing consoles and gear racks as laptop stands.

While this seemingly tectonic shift didn’t put studios out of business, it definitely opened the floodgates for millions of wannabe music producers. Musicians and bands with the right budget still book studio sessions and hire engineers to record, mix, and master music. But do you really **need** outboard gear to mix a song? Can you do it properly without it? Questions like these still circulate in the industry, raised at every other workshop.

## TL;DR
In short, **yes**, you can. Without any exaggeration, it is entirely possible not only to mix, but also to produce a hit song 100% in the box (i.e., on a computer). After all, the term “bedroom producer” is there for a reason.

So, why all the fuss then? Eh, that’s where it gets nuanced. The mixing process is full of variables that create context and affect the result, one way or another. Let’s look at some of the most impactful ones: 
- The space you’re in.
- Your sound source (speakers or headphones).
- The tools you use. 
- Your music production skills and habits.
- Your music taste.

The catch here is that it’s not the signal that gets affected by all of this. **It is you**.

## Space

When in a studio, you are likely in a room that’s been acoustically treated, i.e., designed to translate the sound coming from the speakers as accurately as possible. Professional recording/mixing rooms put you **inside** the arrangement you’re working with, making it easier to hear the faintest sound in the mix and therefore, make the right calls.

## Source of sound
A properly treated room can’t fully serve its purpose without an audio interface (sound card) and studio monitors (speakers). Either come in a great variety, offering different frequency response, power consumption, portability, bit depth, range, etc. Yet, while these characteristics matter, an untreated room will render even the top-end speakers practically useless, turning mixing into a guessing game. When you spot a producer working with such an unbalanced setup, know that they most likely will take their project to a studio later.

A common way to remove space from the picture is to mix in headphones. Keep in mind that it comes with a price, though. While canceling the outside noise, headphones can also eliminate the natural context in which listeners will play the song.

## Tools
Anything you find useful in achieving balance in the mix is technically a mixing tool. Music producers of the past didn’t have infinite compressors or equalizers. They often had to record the sound creatively so it fit the arrangement without mixing. In this so-called analog domain, almost every production decision is a commitment. How the band performed that exact day, how the engineer configured the mixing console that specific session - that’s how it was printed, i.e., recorded on tape. Defects in the output could easily mean re-recording the whole song.

A DAW, however, allows producers to safely experiment with their recordings indefinitely, anywhere and anytime. Just toss your favorite takes into a sequencer, go ham with the effects and plugins, and see what sticks. Add 100 instances of the same plugin, see what it does. Save a copy of your project and pitch everything down 3 semitones just out of curiosity. This reversible, in a way limitless, non-destructive nature of working in a digital domain is what makes DAWs so attractive.

## Skills and habits
As you put years into the craft, you obtain skills and form habits. You learn to deconstruct and analyze other people’s mixes, observe industry veterans, and try applying their best practices to your projects. Again and again, hopefully, getting better at it.

Sooner or later, you’ll notice patterns in your approach, like compressing the drums before even hearing them. These habits usually work until they don’t. Once you catch yourself questioning such a pattern, it’s time for a change.

Growing as a mixing engineer (and as a creative individual) involves going through a never-ending cycle of forming habits and breaking them in favor of the new ones. That’s how you gain experience. Whether it happens on a console or in a DAW is secondary here.

## Taste
Your daily playlist greatly impacts your mixes. Once you get better at analyzing music, you’ll be able to zoom in on what exactly makes this or that song your favorite: the oompf of the kick drum, the power of the guitar riff, or the overall tonal balance. These elements of excitement will eventually shape your mixing style - you’ll keep trying to recreate them in your work, no matter the genre. For example, hip-hop or EDM fans are likely to try their go-to mixing techniques on a metal song, and vice versa.

In music, sound is king, so if it works, it works. Rick Rubin is a prominent example of how important taste is. Notice how his perspective has almost nothing to do with gear or mixing tools. When you see a musician or a band working with seemingly random mixing engineers, chances are, something isn’t clicking, and they are still searching for the right one.

## So?
Mixing music is an art of achieving balance that provokes emotions. It is about the engineer’s decisions, their skills, and taste more than anything else. Analog gear is often praised for bringing warmth and fullness to the signal. Yet people still run endless comparison tests: DAW vs DAW, plugin vs plugin, emulator vs emulator, and so on. Some believe it’s about the passive compression, some say it’s the signal summing, but there is still no concrete conclusion. Yeah, it’s **that** subjective.

Personally, I believe the real magic is in how **non-visual** the analog/outboard workflow is compared to DAWs and plugins. When you can’t **see**, you are forced to **hear**. More of your decisions depend on how the signal **sounds**, not on how it **looks**. If it takes an effort to hook up another compressor, you are more likely to get the most out of the one you already engaged. Every move now has to come with an intention. That’s exactly where you want to be mentally.

Thankfully, you can take the best from both worlds without breaking the bank. To embrace the limitations of the outboard workflow, I removed a computer from my production and mixing: now, it’s just my favorite instruments and a pair of trusty headphones. When I’m done, I _commit_ to my decisions and record the mix (just like the studio would); then I bring it into a DAW for the final tweaks. To stay focused on **hearing** the signal, I stick to plugins with little to no visual reference. At the end of the day, the analog/outboard mindset boosted my creativity, and the digital mindset gave me enough freedom to experiment stress-free.

So, to decide whether you should stay “in the box”, go DAW-less, or figure out something in between, there’s no better way than trying as many setups and tools as possible for yourself. Find an opportunity to spend time in a professional recording/mixing room and listen to your favorite songs there. See how working on a console makes you **feel**. Compare your studio and bedroom mixes and analyze your mixing decisions. Try mixing without plugins at all. Connect with like-minded producers and exchange feedback. See what sets your approaches apart.

Always put balance, perception/mindset, and emotion first. The tools will follow.
